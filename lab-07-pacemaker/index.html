Pacemaker

Idea

What if the pacing curve built the level? Level designers sketch tension as a line: build-up, a peak, a breather, the next peak. Here that line is the level. Each stretch of the curve becomes a room: calm stretches turn into wide, warm rooms, tense ones get narrow, dark and crowded, every dip becomes a save room and the highest peak becomes the boss arena. A runner plays through it and its heart rate follows the tension. Flatten the curve and the monitor flatlines.

Controls

  • Press and drag across the pacing curve to redraw it. The dungeon rebuilds as you draw.
  • Slow burn, Waves, All action and Flat load example curves. The chip under the heart rate checks the pacing and names the rooms with a problem.
  • Pause stops the runner. On the curve, the left and right arrows pick a point and up and down change its tension. Space pauses.

Inspired by

  • The "Elevation Lab" of a game pipeline tool shown on X, where dragging a curve reshapes a world in real time. Only the principle of a curve that rebuilds a world live was taken.
  • Gustav Freytag's pyramid (1863): rising action, climax and falling action as one drawn shape.
  • The interest curve from Jesse Schell's book The Art of Game Design (2008), which plots how engaging an experience is moment by moment.

For game UI

Pacing usually lives in a design doc while the level lives in the editor. Putting the curve inside the tool closes that gap: rooms can tag themselves as breathers or peaks, and simple checks catch a long stretch with no save room or a flat section before a playtest does. The same pattern fits a wave planner for a tower defense game, a difficulty ramp for a puzzle game or the beat sheet of a narrative scene.
